Guide · Harvest

When are olives harvested?

Harvest timing influences yield, aroma, the balance of bitterness and pungency, and the phenolic profile, but is not a quality guarantee by itself.

Olivamore Editorial · sourcing standard24 August 20267 min read
In brief

“Early harvest” and “ripe harvest” describe choices about fruit maturity, not legal quality grades. Healthy olives harvested at either stage can produce extra virgin oil when they are handled carefully, milled promptly and stored well.

Harvest is a balance, not one calendar date

The best timing varies with cultivar, region, temperature, rainfall, crop load, fruit health and the sensory style the producer wants. Skin colour alone is not enough. Oil accumulation, phenolic and sensory monitoring, weather and mill logistics all form part of the decision.

The usual early-harvest profile

Earlier-picked olives commonly produce green fruitiness and notes reminiscent of grass, leaf, tomato or artichoke, with more evident bitterness and pungency. Many cultivars tend to have more phenolics but a lower extractable oil yield at this stage. These are tendencies, not universal outcomes.

The usual ripe-harvest profile

As ripening advances, the profile often becomes milder, with ripe-fruit, almond-like or sweeter-perceived notes, while oil yield may rise. This does not mean lower quality. Sound fruit, gentle handling and prompt milling may matter more than the words on the harvest label.

Why the hours after picking matter

Compression in sacks, bruising, soil contact and long delays can encourage unwanted fermentation. Clean, ventilated crates and rapid delivery to the mill are good practices, but a single promise such as “within 24 hours” is not a quality guarantee by itself.

How to choose at the table

Robust early-harvest oils can give tomatoes, pulses, grilled vegetables and intense meze a strong finish. Milder ripe-harvest oils are an approachable starting point for stews, delicate vegetables and everyday oven cooking. These are pairing hypotheses to test with the actual oil and dish, not fixed rules.

Practical checklist

  • ●Look for the harvest year and cultivar together.
  • ●Do not treat “early harvest” as a laboratory result.
  • ●Do not use green colour as proof of quality.
  • ●Start with a small dose and adjust to the dish.
  • ●Reassess the same product with every new harvest.
Important note

“Early harvest is always better or healthier”, “ripe harvest is a lower grade” and “green colour proves early harvest” are inaccurate generalisations.
